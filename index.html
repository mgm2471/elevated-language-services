Why It Matters

The cost of getting interpretation wrong is not abstract.

Every year, people are misdiagnosed, lose legal cases, have informed consent violated, and in some cases lose their lives, because language access was treated as optional, improvised, or automated. This is documented. These are not hypotheticals.

01

Family members and bilingual staff are not interpreters.

Speaking a language and interpreting it are entirely different skills. Trained interpreters understand specialized terminology, cultural nuance, register, ethics, and the legal obligation of accuracy. A bilingual nurse or a patient's adult child does not.

Research published in Pediatrics found that ad-hoc interpreters make 22% more errors with direct clinical consequences than trained professionals.

There's also a privacy dimension often overlooked: patients routinely withhold sensitive information, mental health, domestic violence, sexual health, when a family member is in the room interpreting.

Legal exposure: ACA Section 1557 and Title VI require qualified interpreters. Using family or untrained staff can result in malpractice liability and loss of federal funding.

02

AI translation is not interpretation, and it is not safe in high-stakes settings.

Google Translate, ChatGPT, and similar tools recognize patterns in text. They do not understand context, register, cultural meaning, or the weight of what is being communicated.

A 2023 ACL study found clinically harmful errors, including potentially life-threatening ones, in machine translations of patient discharge instructions. The National Center for State Courts states courts should never use machine translation in courtroom proceedings.

In Garner v. Kadince (Utah, 2025), attorneys were sanctioned after AI translated "motion in limine" as the nonexistent "motion to eliminate" and generated six entirely fictitious case citations. OpenAI's own policies explicitly bar ChatGPT from use in "high-risk government decision-making including law enforcement, criminal justice, migration and asylum."

AI tools also create PHI exposure: many public tools store inputs to improve their models. Routing patient information through them may constitute a HIPAA violation.

03

Large-volume platforms trade accountability for scale.

High-volume OPI and VRI platforms connect you to whoever is available in the queue. Credentials vary widely and are often unverified. There is no direct relationship, no named professional you can hold accountable.

Regional variation matters. A platform that cannot match interpreters by dialect introduces exactly the kind of miscommunication risk it claims to prevent, the documented cause of the most expensive medical interpretation error in U.S. history.

Ask any large-volume provider: can you guarantee the same certified interpreter across appointments? Can you guarantee dialect matching for indigenous-language-adjacent Spanish? The answer is no.

$71M

Settlement. One word. One missing interpreter.

Willie Ramirez. Florida, 1980. The most expensive medical interpretation error in U.S. history.

An 18-year-old arrived at a South Florida hospital unconscious. His Spanish-speaking family told doctors he was “intoxicado”, a Cuban Spanish term meaning he had ingested something bad, likely food poisoning. The word was interpreted as “intoxicated.” Doctors treated him for a drug overdose. He had a cerebral hemorrhage. For two days, the hemorrhage went untreated. Willie Ramirez left the hospital quadriplegic.

The hospital settled for $71 million. It was established in the lawsuit that, had a neurosurgeon been called when he arrived, Willie Ramirez could have walked out. The word “intoxicado” does not mean “intoxicated” in Cuban Spanish. A professional interpreter with regional competency would have known this immediately.

This is one case. It is the most documented. It is not the only one.

Source: Health Affairs, The Professional Interpreter, Boostlingo. Case documented in Gail Price-Wise, Language, Culture, and Medical Tragedy.

Approach

“Every interpretation ensures that language differences don't become power imbalances.”

Language justice is not a credential. It's a commitment. It means showing up prepared, staying present, maintaining strict confidentiality, and holding to professional ethics even when no one is checking. It means treating every person, regardless of their primary language, as someone whose words carry full weight.

That commitment is why I maintain NCIHC and CCHI ethics adherence on every assignment. It's why I have a formal Safe AI Policy. It's why I provide a HIPAA BAA as standard, not because a client asked, but because it's the right way to do business.

Ultra marathon, ultra OCR, trail. Featured on CNN Fit Nation (2016) and 60 Minutes Sports.

It started with short Spartan Sprint events, the kind where you finish covered in mud and feeling like you found something. Over the next twelve years it evolved into something else entirely: a professional ultra endurance career spanning 50+ competitive races, 30+ podiums, world championship placements, and a course record at one of Colorado's most demanding 100-milers.

The progression followed a deliberate arc, shorter and faster to longer and more brutal, from stadium sprints through Spartan Supers and Beasts, into the Ultra Beast circuit, and finally into pure ultra marathon distance events where the obstacles aren't built by race directors but by the San Juan Mountains themselves. By 2022, that arc reached its clearest expression: the Ouray 100, 102 miles, nearly 42,000 feet of climbing, finished in 30 hours and 27 seconds, a course record that stood more than 45 minutes faster than what had come before.

“This sport has given me the ability to be comfortable with being uncomfortable. I don’t fear adversity now. I feel I can overcome anything, especially with a good attitude.”

None of this happened separately from the work. The two careers ran in parallel, interpretation sessions in the morning, trail miles in the afternoon, races on weekends. The discipline required for both is more similar than it might appear. Ultra endurance competition demands a specific kind of mental architecture: the ability to perform accurately and under control when you are deeply fatigued, to stay precise when everything in you wants to simplify, to have prepared so thoroughly that when conditions get difficult you are not improvising.

A simultaneous interpreter in hour three of a conference, managing register, accuracy, and emotional neutrality under pressure, is drawing on something not entirely different from what carries a runner through hour twenty-eight of a mountain hundred. The preparation is different. The demand on the mind is not.
